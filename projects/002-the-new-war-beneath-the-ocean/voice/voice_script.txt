Just after midnight on February 1, 2026, a Japanese drilling ship began lowering a pipe into the Pacific.

One thousand metres.

Three thousand.

Then five thousand five hundred and sixty-nine metres below the surface.

At that depth, there is no sunlight. The pressure is crushing. The water is close to freezing. And the mud beneath the ship had been isolated from the human world for millions of years.

The vessel was called Chikyu. It was not searching for oil.

It was looking for a way out.

For three days, Japan’s marine science agency pumped rare-earth-rich mud from beneath the seabed near Minamitorishima, a remote island in Japan’s exclusive economic zone. The test did not prove that commercial mining would be profitable. It did not end Japan’s dependence on foreign processing. And it did not open a new mine.

But it proved that material could be lifted continuously from more than five kilometres below the Pacific.

That single fact changes the question.

The ocean floor is no longer only a place scientists can study.

It is becoming a place governments believe they may need to control.

Rare earths are hidden inside some of the most important machines in modern life. They help permanent magnets turn electric motors, guide precision robotics, power wind turbines, move aircraft systems and operate technologies used in defence.

The minerals themselves are not always geologically rare. The difficult part is separating them, refining them and turning them into high-performance magnets.

And that is where the world has a problem.

In 2024, China produced about sixty percent of the world’s magnet rare earths. Its share of refining was about ninety-one percent. Its share of sintered permanent-magnet production was approximately ninety-four percent.

A supply chain can begin in one country, cross several borders and still end at the same bottleneck.

In 2025, Chinese export controls turned that concentration into a physical problem. Shipments of several heavy rare earths and related magnets fell sharply. Manufacturers outside China struggled to secure components. Some reduced production. Others paid a premium for material from alternative suppliers.

The lesson was uncomfortable.

Owning a deposit is not the same as controlling a supply chain.

A country can discover minerals beneath its own territory and still depend on someone else to separate them, refine them and manufacture the parts that industry actually needs.

That is why Japan’s test matters.

The mud near Minamitorishima is not the same resource being discussed in most debates about deep-sea mining. It lies inside Japan’s exclusive economic zone. It contains rare-earth elements in fine sediment beneath the seabed. Japan is developing a closed-loop lifting system intended to reduce leakage as the material rises.

The economics are still unproven. The processing route is still being tested. A larger production-chain trial is planned for 2027, followed by technical and economic assessment.

But the political meaning is already clear.

Japan is not simply looking for minerals.

It is trying to reduce the power of a chokepoint.

And it is not alone.

Thousands of kilometres away, another mineral frontier stretches across the Pacific between Hawaii and Mexico.

The Clarion–Clipperton Zone is a vast abyssal plain. Across parts of its sediment lie polymetallic nodules: dark, potato-sized rocks that formed over millions of years, layer by microscopic layer.

They contain manganese, nickel, copper and cobalt.

Unlike Japan’s rare-earth mud, these nodules often lie in areas beyond national jurisdiction. No country owns the seabed there in the ordinary sense.

Under the United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, the seabed beyond national jurisdiction is described as the common heritage of mankind.

The International Seabed Authority was created to organise and control mineral activities in that area.

It has issued exploration contracts.

But exploration is not commercial extraction.

As of July 24, 2026, the Authority had still not completed the regulations intended to govern exploitation. Negotiators were working through environmental standards, compliance, inspections, financial terms, benefit sharing, effective control, closure plans and the question of what happens when a contractor fails.

This is not a minor delay in paperwork.

These rules are supposed to answer questions humanity has never answered at industrial scale.

How much damage is acceptable in an ecosystem that may recover over centuries?

Who pays if a company collapses after disturbing thousands of square kilometres?

How are benefits shared from a place international law says belongs to humanity as a whole?

And who has the authority to say no?

While those negotiations continue, the United States is building a parallel route.

The United States has not ratified the Law of the Sea Convention. Instead, it regulates deep-seabed mineral activity by United States companies under a domestic law known as the Deep Seabed Hard Mineral Resources Act.

In 2026, NOAA revised its process so exploration and commercial recovery applications could move through a consolidated review structure.

Several applications entered public review.

But no United States commercial recovery permit had been issued.

That distinction matters.

An application is not approval.

A certified application is not a mine.

And a political order to accelerate permitting does not erase the engineering, financial, environmental and legal barriers between a plan and an operating industry.

Still, the direction is unmistakable.

The world now has one ocean, two rulebooks and no commercial precedent.

The first operation that crosses that line may do more than collect minerals.

It may establish who gets to define the rules after the fact.

Supporters of deep-sea mining argue that waiting also has consequences.

Modern infrastructure requires enormous quantities of material. Copper demand is rising. Rare-earth supply chains remain highly concentrated. Some terrestrial mines destroy forests, generate tailings, consume water and displace communities.

From that perspective, collecting nodules from an uninhabited abyssal plain could appear less destructive than opening new mines on land.

The argument cannot be dismissed simply because some of its strongest advocates are mining companies.

Every mineral has to come from somewhere.

A wind turbine is not weightless.

An electric grid is not built from policy targets.

A robot, a data centre, a missile guidance system and an electric motor all depend on physical materials extracted, processed and transported through the real world.

But the phrase “we need deep-sea mining for the energy transition” is too simple.

Battery chemistry is changing. Lithium-iron-phosphate batteries use neither nickel nor cobalt. Recycling will grow. New terrestrial projects may increase supply. Demand forecasts move with technology, price and policy.

And for rare earths, the most important bottleneck may not be the mine.

It may be the refinery.

Moving extraction to the ocean does not automatically move separation, metal production or magnet manufacturing out of the dominant supply chain.

The strategic value of seabed minerals may therefore be different from their market value.

A country may be willing to pay more for a difficult source if it reduces dependence during a crisis.

That is not ordinary mining economics.

It is insurance.

And insurance is often purchased because of what might happen, not because it is the cheapest option today.

The environmental problem is equally resistant to slogans.

The deep ocean is not empty.

It is dark, slow and poorly understood.

Many organisms live on, inside or around the sediment. Some attach to nodules that took millions of years to form. Remove the nodules and the hard surface they provide does not return on a human timescale.

A study published in 2025 revisited tracks left by a small collector test carried out in 1979.

Forty-four years later, the physical disturbance was still visible. Many biological communities remained altered.

But the site was not completely lifeless.

Some mobile organisms and sediment fauna had begun to recolonise.

That is the honest result.

Recovery was not zero.

Recovery was not complete.

And a small experimental machine from the nineteen seventies is not the same as a modern commercial fleet operating continuously.

A separate study examined a much larger collector trial that recovered more than three thousand tonnes of nodules.

Two months after the test, macrofaunal density inside the mining tracks was thirty-seven percent lower. Species richness was thirty-two percent lower.

Outside the direct tracks, the effects of the sediment plume were more complicated. Abundance did not fall in the same way, but the relationships between dominant species changed.

Again, the result is not a slogan.

The clearest near-term damage was where the machine physically crossed the seabed.

Plume effects were subtler, but not absent.

And two months cannot tell us what recovery looks like after ten years, fifty years or a century.

Sediment plumes are one of the most contested parts of the debate.

Measurements from collector trials show that disturbed sediment can travel kilometres. The highest concentrations often remain close to the seabed and decline with distance, height and time.

That does not mean the plume stays beside the machine.

It also does not support the claim that one collector will poison an entire ocean.

The unresolved issue is accumulation.

One test lasts days.

A commercial operation could involve multiple collectors, repeated passes, surface processing and discharge over years.

Noise, light, suspended particles and the removal of nodules would not occur once.

They would become a new background condition.

The midwater ocean creates another uncertainty.

Material brought to the surface must be separated from seawater and sediment. If waste particles are discharged back into the ocean, they may enter food webs built around the slow fall of natural organic matter.

Research suggests those discharge particles may be nutritionally poor compared with the particles many midwater organisms normally consume.

That identifies a plausible mechanism of harm.

It does not prove that a commercial operation would collapse an ecosystem.

But it reveals how easily a decision made at the surface can alter a system far below it.

The strongest case against rushing is not that science already knows every consequence.

It is that science does not.

The strongest case for moving ahead is not that the damage will be harmless.

It is that the alternatives also carry damage, and that mineral dependence can become a national-security risk.

Both arguments contain truth.

That is what makes the decision dangerous.

If deep-sea mining were obviously catastrophic, the choice would be easy.

If it were obviously safe and profitable, the race would already be over.

Instead, governments are being asked to make a precedent-setting decision while the economics, technology, law and ecology are all still moving.

The first commercial project will not simply answer whether a machine can collect minerals.

It will answer what kind of uncertainty is enough to stop an industry.

It will reveal whether international rules can govern a frontier before powerful states create alternatives.

And it will test whether the phrase “common heritage of mankind” has practical meaning when the resource beneath it becomes strategically valuable.

There is another possibility.

The most important outcome of this race may not be a mine.

Japan’s effort could fail economically. Nodule prices could make commercial projects unattractive. New magnet technologies could reduce demand. Recycling and diversified refining could weaken the security argument.

Governments may spend billions to create options they never fully use.

But even an unused option changes power.

A state that can credibly say it has another source negotiates differently from one that has none.

That is why the contest is beginning before commercial production.

Survey ships, permits, pilot systems, processing research and legal claims are all ways of reserving a place in a future that may or may not arrive.

The seabed is becoming strategic long before it becomes profitable.

And the danger is that the world may mistake momentum for inevitability.

The ability to reach a resource does not create an obligation to extract it.

But delay is not neutral either.

Every year without diversified processing leaves industries exposed to concentrated supply.

Every year without agreed international rules gives national alternatives more room to become the default.

And every year without baseline ecological research makes it harder to know what was lost after industrial activity begins.

The decision is not between progress and preservation.

It is between different forms of risk, distributed across different people, places and generations.

Land communities may bear the cost of terrestrial mining.

Manufacturers may bear the cost of supply disruption.

Companies and states may capture the strategic benefit.

The deep ocean may carry damage that cannot be seen from shore.

And future generations may inherit the precedent.

In 2027, Japan plans to return to the Pacific for a longer production-chain test.

More applications will move through national processes.

The International Seabed Authority will continue negotiating a mining code.

Scientists will return to old tracks and new experiments, trying to measure recovery in a world where the commercial decision may arrive before the final answer.

The race is real.

But it is not only a race for minerals.

It is a race to decide which rules will exist when extraction becomes possible.

Who carries the uncertainty.

Who receives the benefit.

And whether the first country or company to move will define the frontier for everyone who comes after.

Six kilometres below the Pacific, the mud is still there.

The nodules are still there.

The law is still unfinished.

The deepest part of this story is not the distance from the surface to the seabed.

It is the distance between what humanity has learned how to extract...

and what it has agreed not to destroy.
