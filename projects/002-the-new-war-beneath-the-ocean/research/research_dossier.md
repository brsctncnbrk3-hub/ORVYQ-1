## 1. Answer-first finding

The strongest version of this film is not “deep-sea mining: good or bad?”

It is a story about **control**.

Japan has now demonstrated that rare-earth mud can be lifted continuously from more than five kilometres below the Pacific. The United States is accelerating a national permit route for mineral recovery in international waters. China remains the dominant force in rare-earth refining and permanent-magnet manufacturing. Meanwhile, the International Seabed Authority still has not completed the commercial exploitation rules intended to govern the seabed beyond national jurisdiction.

The result is a race in which engineering and national-security policy are moving faster than shared law, ecological knowledge and proven commercial economics.

**Working thesis:**  
The new contest for the ocean floor is being driven as much by the desire to escape mineral chokepoints as by resource demand itself. The first commercial decision may therefore become a precedent for who controls a frontier that international law calls the common heritage of mankind.

## 2. Why this story is timely

### The trigger event

On 1 February 2026, Japan's drilling vessel *Chikyu* began continuously lifting rare-earth mud through a pipe system reaching a depth of 5,569 metres in the Minamitorishima exclusive economic zone. JAMSTEC later confirmed recovery from three rare-earth layers during a three-day test.

Japan plans a larger, approximately one-month production-chain trial beginning in February 2027. The programme intends to move material to Minamitorishima, dewater it, transport it to the Japanese mainland and test separation, refining and smelting. A combined technical and economic assessment is targeted by March 2028.

This is a stronger opening than a generic shot of polymetallic nodules because it is:
- current;
- state-led;
- visibly difficult;
- directly tied to economic security;
- legally distinct from mining in international waters.

### The strategic pressure

The IEA reports that China held approximately:
- 60% of magnet rare-earth mining in 2024;
- 91% of magnet rare-earth refining;
- 94% of sintered permanent-magnet production.

Export controls introduced in 2025 caused sharp supply interruptions and downstream production problems outside China. That makes Japan's Minamitorishima programme a supply-chain-security project, not merely a mining experiment.

### The legal pressure

UNCLOS describes the seabed beyond national jurisdiction as the **common heritage of mankind** and assigns the International Seabed Authority a central role in organizing and controlling mineral activities there.

Yet, as of the end of the ISA Council meetings on 24 July 2026, the commercial exploitation regulations were still unfinished. Delegates were assessing remaining work, a roadmap and further intersessional negotiations.

At the same time, the United States—which has not ratified UNCLOS—has directed agencies to accelerate seabed-mineral permitting under domestic law. NOAA's 2026 framework now allows a consolidated review of exploration and commercial recovery applications. The Metals Company's application has passed procedural compliance milestones, but no commercial recovery permit has yet been issued.

This produces the film's central geopolitical fracture:

> One ocean. Two rulebooks. No commercial precedent yet.

## 3. The essential distinction the film must preserve

“Deep-sea mining” is not one thing.

### A. Rare-earth mud near Minamitorishima
- Inside Japan's exclusive economic zone.
- Fine sediment below the seabed.
- Target value is rare-earth elements, especially strategically important medium and heavy rare earths.
- Extraction concept uses a closed-loop riser system intended to limit suspended sediment leakage.
- Legal authority is primarily national because the site is within Japan's EEZ.
- Commercial economics remain unproven.

### B. Polymetallic nodules in the Clarion–Clipperton Zone
- Mostly in areas beyond national jurisdiction.
- Potato-sized nodules resting on abyssal sediment.
- Rich in manganese, nickel, copper and cobalt, with some other elements.
- Collected by large tracked machines and pumped to surface vessels.
- International governance is centered on the ISA for UNCLOS parties.
- The United States is pursuing a parallel DSHMRA route.

### C. Cobalt-rich crusts and seafloor massive sulphides
- Different geology, ecosystems and mining methods.
- Often associated with seamounts or hydrothermal-vent systems.
- They should appear only as context, not be treated as equivalent to nodule mining.

This distinction is critical for credibility. The film must never transfer a result from one deposit type, legal area or mining method to another without explanation.

## 4. The mineral-security argument — and its limitation

The strongest honest argument for seabed mining is not that every green technology will fail without it.

The 2026 IEA outlook shows a more complicated picture:
- demand for critical minerals continues to rise;
- copper and rare-earth supply-chain risks remain serious;
- cobalt demand growth has moderated because lithium-iron-phosphate batteries use no cobalt or nickel;
- announced and early-stage nickel projects could cover demand in a high-production case;
- processing and refining concentration can be a bigger vulnerability than the geological location of ore;
- recycling will expand, but not fast enough to erase near-term concentration risk.

That means the film should challenge the phrase “we need seabed minerals for the energy transition.”

A more accurate formulation is:

> Some minerals may face supply pressure. But the race is also about where refining happens, who controls technology, who can restrict exports and which states are willing to pay a strategic premium for independence.

This nuance makes Japan's rare-earth mud more narratively compelling than a simple battery-metals story.

## 5. What the environmental evidence actually says

### Long-term physical disturbance

A 2025 *Nature* study revisited tracks left by a small 1979 collector test. After 44 years:
- physical disturbance was still visible;
- many communities remained altered;
- some mobile organisms and sediment fauna had begun to recolonize;
- the old machine and small footprint were not identical to a modern commercial operation.

Correct interpretation:
- recovery is not zero;
- recovery is not complete;
- directly disturbed abyssal habitat can remain changed for decades.

### Industrial-scale track effects

A 2026 *Nature Ecology & Evolution* study examined a 2022 trial that recovered more than 3,000 tonnes of nodules. Two months after the test:
- macrofaunal density was 37% lower within mining tracks;
- species richness was 32% lower within mining tracks;
- plume-affected sites did not show the same abundance decline, but species-dominance relationships changed.

Correct interpretation:
- the most clearly measured near-term damage was inside the collector tracks;
- plume effects were subtler but not absent;
- two months is too short to determine recovery.

### Sediment plumes

A 2025 *Nature Communications* monitoring study found:
- sediment moved kilometres from a collector trial;
- the highest concentrations remained close to the seabed;
- concentrations fell rapidly with distance, height and time;
- redeposited sediment reached centimetre scale near tracks;
- commercial cumulative effects remain uncertain.

Correct interpretation:
- “the plume stays local” is too simple;
- “the plume poisons the entire Pacific” is also unsupported;
- scale, thresholds, repeated operations and cumulative exposure are the unresolved questions.

### Midwater discharge

Another 2025 *Nature Communications* study found that proposed discharge particles are nutritionally poor compared with the natural particles supporting midwater food webs. The study identifies a plausible disruption mechanism for particle feeders and their predators.

Correct interpretation:
- this is a serious pathway;
- it is not direct proof of commercial-scale ecosystem collapse;
- discharge design and depth matter.

## 6. The industry's strongest counterargument

Proponents argue that collecting nodules could avoid:
- deforestation;
- relocation of communities;
- conventional open pits;
- large mine tailings;
- some greenhouse-gas and waste impacts of terrestrial ore extraction.

Those arguments should appear in the film. Excluding them would make the documentary one-sided.

However:
- many lifecycle assessments are company-commissioned or depend on projected processing systems;
- a lifecycle advantage in climate or waste does not automatically compensate for habitat loss in a poorly understood ecosystem;
- land mines vary greatly, so “land mining” is not a single comparison;
- future battery chemistry and commodity prices can change the value of the nodule basket;
- the location of refining may remain a strategic bottleneck even if extraction moves offshore.

The film should present this as a real trade-off, not a rhetorical straw man.

## 7. Recommended story architecture

### Chapter 1 — The mud that reached daylight
Open with *Chikyu* at sea, pipe sections descending and the first material arriving from 5,569 metres.

**Question:** Why would a country attempt something this difficult?

### Chapter 2 — The chokepoint
Reveal the concentration of rare-earth refining and magnet production. Show how export controls transformed dependence into a national-security concern.

**Turn:** The ocean floor is not valuable only because of what is there. It is valuable because of who controls what is already above ground.

### Chapter 3 — The black map
Move from Japan's EEZ to the Clarion–Clipperton Zone. Contract blocks appear across a dark Pacific map. Explain nodules, contractors and the difference between exploration and exploitation.

**Question:** Who owns minerals beyond any nation's border?

### Chapter 4 — Two rulebooks
Place UNCLOS Articles 136–137 against the U.S. executive order and NOAA permit pathway. Explain the unfinished ISA mining code and the procedural status of TMC's application.

**Turn:** The first mine may not wait for the world's rulebook.

### Chapter 5 — What the machine leaves behind
Use real track photographs, plume maps and species data. Keep limitations on screen.

**Question:** How do you define acceptable damage in an ecosystem that recovers on geological timescales?

### Chapter 6 — The argument for mining
Present land-mining harms, lifecycle claims, changing battery chemistry, recycling and processing concentration.

**Turn:** The choice is not ocean damage versus no damage. It is which damage, where, for which minerals, and under whose control.

### Chapter 7 — The precedent
Return to the first recovered mud and to the blank spaces in the law.

**Ending idea:**  
The deepest part of this race is not six kilometres below the Pacific. It is the distance between what humanity can extract and what it has agreed not to destroy.

## 8. Recommended opening

### Visual sequence
1. Black screen; low mechanical vibration.
2. Wide shot of *Chikyu* in rough water.
3. Pipe counter or depth graphic: 1,000 m → 3,000 m → 5,569 m.
4. ROV lights on an otherwise black seabed.
5. Mud entering the surface system.
6. Cut to a clean magnet, missile actuator, EV motor and surgical scanner.
7. Map line from Minamitorishima to China-centered refining hubs.

### Narration direction
Do not begin with “The ocean covers 70% of Earth.”

Begin with a concrete action:

> Just after midnight on February 1, 2026, a Japanese ship began pulling mud from a place sunlight has never reached.

Then reveal the stakes:

> It was not looking for oil. It was looking for a way out.

This wording is a direction, not final locked narration.

## 10. Claims that must be carefully worded

### Do not say
- “Japan found enough rare earths to end dependence on China.”
- “The United States has approved commercial deep-sea mining.”
- “The ISA has legalized commercial mining.”
- “The deep ocean never recovers.”
- “Sediment plumes remain only near the machine.”
- “Deep-sea mining is required for electric vehicles.”
- “All deep-sea mining happens in international waters.”

### Use instead
- Japan demonstrated recovery technology and plans an integrated trial; reserve size and economics remain unresolved.
- TMC's U.S. application passed procedural milestones; no commercial recovery permit had been issued as of the research lock.
- ISA exploitation rules remained unfinished as of 24 July 2026.
- Some recolonization occurs, while direct physical and biological impacts can persist for decades.
- Plumes can travel kilometres but measured concentrations vary strongly with distance and height.
- Mineral need is chemistry- and supply-chain-specific.
- National EEZ projects and international-seabed projects operate under different legal regimes.
